Diabetes Fact Sheet
Source: World Health Organization — Diabetes fact sheet

Key Facts:
- The number of people living with diabetes rose from 200 million in 1990 to 830 million in 2022, rising faster in low- and middle-income countries.
- More than half of people with diabetes were not taking medication for it in 2022.
- Diabetes can cause blindness, kidney failure, heart attack, stroke, and lower-limb amputation.
- In 2021, diabetes and related kidney disease caused over 2 million deaths, and about 11% of cardiovascular deaths were linked to high blood glucose.
- A healthy diet, regular activity, a healthy body weight, and avoiding tobacco can prevent or delay type 2 diabetes.

Overview:
Diabetes is a chronic condition where the pancreas doesn't produce enough insulin, or the body can't use the insulin it produces effectively. This leads to raised blood glucose (hyperglycaemia), which over time damages nerves and blood vessels throughout the body. Type 1 diabetes requires daily insulin and has no known prevention. Type 2 diabetes makes up more than 95% of cases, is often preventable, and is linked to excess weight, low physical activity, and genetics. Gestational diabetes occurs during pregnancy and raises future type 2 risk for both mother and child.

Symptoms:
- Feeling very thirsty
- Needing to urinate more often than usual
- Blurred vision
- Persistent fatigue
- Unintentional weight loss

Escalation Note:
Type 2 diabetes symptoms are often mild and can go unnoticed for years, sometimes only diagnosed after complications have already developed — which is why regular checkups and blood tests matter even without obvious symptoms.

Prevention:
- Maintain a healthy body weight
- Get at least 150 minutes of moderate physical activity per week
- Eat a balanced diet, limiting sugar and saturated fat
- Avoid tobacco use
- Get regular checkups and blood glucose testing, especially with a family history of diabetes

Treatment:
Diagnosis is done through blood glucose testing. Type 1 diabetes requires daily insulin injections for survival. Type 2 diabetes is managed through lifestyle changes, and sometimes medication such as metformin, sulfonylureas, or SGLT-2 inhibitors, occasionally alongside insulin. People with diabetes may also need blood-pressure medication, statins, regular foot care, kidney disease screening, and eye exams to catch complications early.
